0D · Point defects & lattice vibrations

Point Defect Lab

Place defects by hand, or switch on thermal equilibrium and heat the crystal to watch vacancies form, hop and anneal.

Thermodynamics

Vacancy calculator (lecture example)

What the lab shows

  • Creating a defect raises the internal energy, but the configurational entropy lowers G = E + PV − TS. G is lowest at a finite number of defects.
  • Equilibrium fraction (Frenkel pairs and ionic Schottky pairs use E/2kBT instead of E/kBT): n/N = e−E/kBT
  • Diffusion: an atom jumps into a neighbouring vacancy at rate p, which sets the diffusion coefficient D: p = ν e−Em/kBT, D = νa² e−E/kBT
  • Quenching freezes in the high-temperature vacancy population; that is why quenched crystals hold more vacancies than equilibrium allows.

1D · Line defects

Edge Dislocation Lab

Apply a shear stress and the extra half-plane glides through the crystal one bond at a time. Run a Burgers circuit, annihilate a ⊥ with a ⊤, or make the dislocation climb.

Burgers circuit

Steps: n down, n right, n up, n left, atom to atom. The vector from the finish F back to the start S is the Burgers vector b.

The rug analogy

Moving a ruck along a rug shifts the whole rug by the ruck length with a small push, while dragging the flat rug needs a large force. A dislocation does the same for a crystal: only the bonds at the line break and re-form.

Kittel Ch. 21 Fig. 6 · Ashcroft & Mermin footnote 18

Key facts

  • Positive edge ⊥: compression above the slip plane, tension below. Negative ⊤: the reverse.
  • b is perpendicular to the edge dislocation line and lies in the slip plane, so the line can glide only on that plane.
  • Passing one dislocation completely across the crystal produces a surface step of one b: the same result as rigid slip, at a tiny fraction of the stress.
  • Climb (motion normal to the slip plane) needs vacancies to arrive at or leave the extra half-plane, so it happens at high temperature.

1D · Screw, mixed & crystal growth

Screw Dislocation & Spiral Growth

Rotate a 3D crystal and turn the character angle from pure screw to pure edge. Then use a screw dislocation's permanent surface step to grow a crystal at tiny supersaturation.

Spiral growth

A screw dislocation leaves a step that never disappears, so new layers never need to nucleate. Rates are schematic (BCF spiral law ∝ σ² vs 2D nucleation ∝ e−σc/σ).

Key facts

  • A screw dislocation turns successive atomic planes into one helical surface; going once around the line moves you up one plane.
  • b ∥ line for screw, b ⟂ line for edge. A mixed dislocation has both components: b cos φ (screw) and b sin φ (edge).
  • A screw dislocation has a pure shear field, τ = Gb/2πr, and no tension or compression.
  • Crystals grow at supersaturations below 1 %, where a perfect face could not nucleate a new layer. Whiskers that contain a single axial screw dislocation approach the ideal strength.

1D · Why crystals are weak, and how to make them strong

Strength, Sources & Work Hardening

Compare Frenkel's ideal shear strength with measured values, run a Frank–Read source, and work-harden a bar until it breaks.

Ideal vs observed shear strength (log scale)

Kittel Table 21.1 (tap a row)

Values in dyn cm⁻²; 1 dyn cm⁻² = 0.1 Pa

A segment pinned at both ends bows under stress. Its radius cannot fall below L/2, so the critical stress is τc ≈ Gb/L. Above it, the line wraps around the pins, the two arms meet and annihilate, a loop is released, and the source starts again.

Kittel Ch. 21 Figs. 17–18

Each bend multiplies and tangles dislocations, which then block each other: the bar becomes harder but less ductile, and finally breaks. Annealing lets dislocations climb and annihilate, restoring softness. The flow stress uses the Taylor relation τ = τ₀ + αGb√ρ. EXTENSION

Presets

The c/h column uses Jagodzinski notation: a layer whose two neighbours are the same letter sits in an hcp-like (h) environment; otherwise fcc-like (c).

During twinning every plane above the twin plane shifts by a fraction of a lattice vector, the shift growing with distance. When complete, the upper region is the mirror image of the lower one. Slip, by contrast, moves a whole block by a full lattice vector on a few widely spaced planes.

Kittel Ch. 21 "Slip" · Ashcroft & Mermin "Stacking faults"

Within each grain all unit cells share one orientation; where grains meet, the mismatch forms a boundary. Smaller grains mean more boundary to block dislocations. The Hall–Petch read-out σy = σ₀ + k/√d uses mild-steel constants. EXTENSION

3D · Volume (bulk) defects

Precipitates, Pores & Cracks

Drive a dislocation through second-phase particles. Small coherent particles are cut; hard ones are bypassed and leave Orowan loops. Then see how pores and cracks concentrate stress.

Age-hardening curve

peak where cutting and bypassing stresses cross

Particles a distance L apart force a dislocation to bow between them; the stress needed is about τ ≈ Gb/L (Kittel Eq. 21.10). Fresh precipitates are small and close together (strong); long ageing coarsens them, L grows and the alloy softens (overageing).

Lecture §5 · Kittel Ch. 21 "Strength of alloys"

Stress concentration at an elliptical hole: Kt = 1 + 2a/c (Inglis). A round pore triples the local stress; a sharp crack multiplies it many times. Pores also remove load-bearing area: σeff ≈ σ/(1 − P). EXTENSION
